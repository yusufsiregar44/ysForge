# Research phases

Detail for the steps in [`../SKILL.md`](../SKILL.md). Read the phases named by the step you are on.

Evidence tiers and alternative data (originally Phases 18–19) live in `SKILL.md` — they govern every run, not one phase.

---

## Phase 0 — Mandate and compliance

Establish whether the investment can be owned before underwriting it.

### Sector screen

Exclude businesses materially involved in conventional banking, conventional lending, conventional insurance, alcohol, tobacco, gambling, adult entertainment, pork, or prohibited weapons-related activities.

### Financial screen

Use an established methodology where possible — AAOIFI, S&P Shariah, MSCI Islamic, FTSE Russell Shariah — and state which. Review interest-bearing debt, cash and interest-bearing securities, and non-permissible income.

For Indonesian equities, verify current inclusion in the relevant Shariah universes (ISSI, JII70, JII). Do not assume historical Shariah status remains current.

### Purification

Flag incidental non-compliant income where purification may be required.

### Non-compliant securities

If clearly non-compliant: state this plainly, explain the specific issue briefly, stop detailed underwriting unless comparison is still useful, and identify the closest compliant alternative.

## Phase 1 — Define the investment question

Convert broad requests into decision-relevant questions.

Avoid: *Research Nvidia.*
Prefer: *At the current valuation, what operating results must Nvidia achieve to generate an attractive 5-year return, and what could cause realized results to differ materially from those expectations?*

Avoid: *Should I diversify into Europe?*
Prefer: *Does adding European equities materially reduce the portfolio's dependence on US equities, USD exposure, technology concentration, and valuation risk while preserving an acceptable expected return?*

## Phase 3 — Initial hypothesis

Create a falsifiable hypothesis before deep research.

```markdown
## Initial Hypothesis

The stock may be undervalued because the market is extrapolating a temporary
margin decline into a structural deterioration.

### What Must Be True

1. Revenue growth remains structurally intact.
2. Margin compression is temporary.
3. Competitive positioning has not deteriorated.
4. Management can restore operating leverage.
5. The recovery is not already reflected in the current valuation.

### What Would Disprove It

- continued market-share losses,
- permanently lower incremental margins,
- deteriorating customer economics,
- destructive capital allocation.
```

## Phase 4 — Decompose the research

For individual equities, default to these modules: business model, industry structure, competitive advantage, market opportunity, unit economics, financial quality, capital allocation, management, market expectations, valuation, catalysts, risks, bear case, historical base rates, portfolio fit, Shariah compliance, exit conditions.

Parallelize substantially independent questions. Sequence questions that depend on prior conclusions.

---

## Phase 5 — Understand the business economically

Do not begin with the stock chart. First establish who pays the company, why customers pay, what determines volume, what determines pricing, what drives gross margin, what creates operating leverage, how much capital must be reinvested, and where free cash flow actually comes from.

Reduce the economics to a simple model where possible:

```text
Revenue  =  Customers × Usage × Price
Revenue  =  Installed Capacity × Utilization × Realized Price
```

Identify the 2–4 variables that dominate intrinsic value.

---

## Phase 6 — Identify the economic engine

Determine whether long-term value creation comes primarily from unit growth, pricing power, operating leverage, recurring revenue, asset turns, capital efficiency, acquisitions, market-share gains, financial leverage, or reinvestment.

> **What variable explains most of the company's future intrinsic-value growth?**

Do not allow dozens of KPIs to obscure the few that matter.

---

## Phase 7 — Industry structure

Research market size, growth, concentration, barriers to entry, customer power, supplier power, substitution risk, pricing structure, regulation, capacity cycles, technological disruption, and capital intensity.

Ask: *is this a strong business inside a structurally weak industry?* Industry economics can overwhelm company execution.

---

## Phase 8 — Competitive advantage

Do not repeat management's moat claims without evidence. Potential advantages: switching costs, network effects, scale, cost advantage, regulatory barriers, distribution, brand, data, ecosystem lock-in, superior process, learning curves.

Test each claimed moat:

```markdown
### Moat

**Claimed Advantage:**  [What is the advantage?]
**Mechanism:**          [Why should it create superior economics?]
**Evidence:**           [What observable data supports it?]
**Duration:**           [Why should it persist?]
**Erosion Risk:**       [What could weaken it?]
```

A genuine moat eventually appears in the economics — sustained ROIC, gross-margin resilience, retention, pricing power, market-share gains, low churn, superior asset turns.

---

## Phase 9 — Financial quality

Use multi-year history. Review revenue growth, organic growth, gross margin, operating margin, free-cash-flow margin, ROIC, incremental ROIC, cash conversion, working capital, capex intensity, leverage, dilution, stock-based compensation, acquisition spending, and dividend sustainability.

Separate structural from cyclical from accounting from one-off. Do not extrapolate unusually strong or weak periods.

### Incremental economics

Historical ROIC is insufficient. Ask: **what return does the company earn on the next dollar of reinvested capital?**

```text
Long-Term Value Creation  ≈  Reinvestment Rate × Incremental Return on Capital
```

Growth does not create value automatically. Growth creates value when incremental returns exceed the cost of capital.

---

## Phase 10 — Management and capital allocation

Evaluate management through actions rather than charisma. Review organic reinvestment, acquisitions, dividends, buybacks, debt, equity issuance, compensation, strategic consistency, and guidance history.

Does management allocate capital intelligently? Are incentives aligned? Does it issue shares cheaply? Are buybacks valuation-sensitive? Have acquisitions created value? Is communication candid about uncertainty? Do actions match stated priorities?

---

## Phase 11 — Market expectations

Mandatory. Separate what is happening from what is already priced in, and build an expectations map — the table is section 7 of [`equity-memo.md`](equity-memo.md).

A differentiated view matters only if the difference is both **material** and **defensible**.

---

## Phase 12 — Valuation

Never state that a security is cheap or expensive without quantifying what that means. Methods, chosen to suit the business: DCF, reverse DCF, P/E, EV/EBIT, EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF, FCF yield, NAV, sum-of-the-parts, normalized earnings, dividend discount, replacement value. Use more than one when it materially improves confidence.

### Reverse valuation

Prefer it where useful. **What operating assumptions must be true to justify the current price?**

Estimate the implied growth, margins, reinvestment, duration, and terminal economics — then judge whether those assumptions are conservative, reasonable, demanding, or implausible.

---

## Phase 13 — Scenario analysis

Construct Bear, Base and Bull at minimum. **Do not vary only the valuation multiple — vary the economics.** Use ranges when precision would mislead. The table is section 9 of [`equity-memo.md`](equity-memo.md).

---

## Phase 14 — Base rates

Before accepting a narrative, compare it against historical precedent:

- How often do companies at this scale maintain this growth rate?
- How long have similar margins historically persisted?
- How often do category leaders retain leadership through technology transitions?
- How often do expensive compounders successfully grow into their valuation?
- How often do cyclical earnings peaks appear structurally permanent?

Base rates do not determine the outcome. They determine the burden of proof.

---

## Phase 15 — Catalysts and milestones

Separate **thesis drivers** (which change intrinsic value) from **catalysts** (which change market perception).

Potential catalysts: earnings inflection, margin recovery, new product launch, regulatory change, deleveraging, capital returns, management change, asset sale, capacity normalization, competitive exit.

Identify observable milestones rather than vague narratives.

---

## Phase 16 — Bear case

Steel-man the strongest argument against owning it:

- Why might the current market price be correct?
- Is apparently temporary weakness actually structural?
- What hidden liability exists?
- What could cause permanent capital impairment?
- Which assumption in the model is weakest?
- What would a sophisticated short seller focus on?

Do not weaken the bear case to preserve the thesis.

---

## Phase 17 — Thesis kill criteria

Define explicit invalidating conditions **before capital is committed**: market share below a threshold, structural gross-margin deterioration, broken customer retention, incremental ROIC below the hurdle rate, excessive leverage, value-destructive M&A, competition destroying pricing power, valuation dependent on implausible assumptions.

---

## Phase 20 — Polymath synthesis

Cross-domain lenses, where they improve the investment model:

- **Economics** — supply and demand, elasticity, market structure, economies of scale, substitution
- **Game theory** — strategic interaction, coordination, deterrence, winner-take-most dynamics
- **History** — technology diffusion, infrastructure cycles, bubbles, industrial transitions
- **Computer science** — protocols, abstraction layers, modularity, platform economics, compute bottlenecks
- **Organizational theory** — incentives, principal-agent problems, bureaucracy, coordination costs
- **Psychology** — narrative formation, herding, extrapolation, anchoring

For every analogy: what maps, where does it break, and does it change the conclusion? If not, omit it.

---

## Phase 21 — Diagnose mispricing

State explicitly why the opportunity may exist: short-termism, forced selling, index mechanics, temporary earnings distortion, cyclicality, poor disclosure, excessive complexity, regulatory uncertainty, management transition, conglomerate discount, accounting misunderstanding, currency distortion, an underfollowed security, narrative dominance.

> **Why does this opportunity exist if the thesis is obvious?**

If there is no plausible answer, reduce confidence.

---

## Phase 22 — Asymmetry

Evaluate payoff, not merely thesis quality. If correct, how much can we make? If wrong, how much can we lose? Is the downside temporary or permanent? How long must capital be committed? What is the opportunity cost?

Prefer understandable downside, materially larger upside, observable milestones, and attractive expected return.
